Mechanical Keyboards: A Beginner's Guide

Mechanical keyboards feel better, last longer, and are more satisfying to type on than the membrane boards most computers ship with. But the jargon is a wall. This guide gets you to a confident first purchase.

Start with the switches

The switch under each key is what defines how a mechanical keyboard feels. There are dozens of variants, but they fall into three families:

  • Linear (often red) — smooth all the way down, no bump. Quiet and fast, popular for gaming. A good neutral starting point if you're unsure.
  • Tactile (often brown) — a small bump partway down tells your finger the key has registered. Many people find these the best all-rounder for typing.
  • Clicky (often blue) — a tactile bump plus an audible click. Deeply satisfying, but loud enough to annoy anyone sharing your room or a video call.

If you can't test switches in person, tactile browns are the safest first choice. Boards with hot-swappable switches let you change them later without soldering — a great feature to look for while you're still figuring out your preference.

Then pick a size

Layout is about how much desk space you want back and whether you need a number pad:

  • Full-size — everything, including the number pad. Best if you work with spreadsheets or numbers all day.
  • Tenkeyless (TKL) — drops the number pad. The most popular size: compact but keeps the arrow keys and function row.
  • 75% and 65% — smaller still, squeezing the keys closer together. Frees up desk space and brings your mouse hand closer, which many people find more comfortable.
