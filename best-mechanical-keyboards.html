Mechanical Keyboards: A Beginner's Guide

Mechanical keyboards feel better, last longer, and are more satisfying to type on than the membrane boards most computers ship with. But the jargon is a wall. This guide gets you to a confident first purchase.

Start with the switches

The switch under each key defines how a mechanical keyboard feels. There are dozens of variants, but they fall into three families:

  • Linear (often red) — smooth all the way down, no bump. Quiet and fast, popular for gaming. A good neutral starting point.
  • Tactile (often brown) — a small bump partway down tells your finger the key registered. The best all-rounder for typing.
  • Clicky (often blue) — a bump plus an audible click. Satisfying, but loud enough to annoy anyone near you.

If you can't test switches in person, tactile browns are the safest first choice. Boards with hot-swappable switches let you change them later without soldering.

Then pick a size

  • Full-size — everything, including the number pad. Best if you work with numbers all day.
  • Tenkeyless (TKL) — drops the number pad. The most popular size: compact but keeps the arrows and function row.
  • 75% and 65% — smaller still, freeing desk space and bringing your mouse hand closer.

A note on keycaps

Keycaps affect feel and longevity. Cheap ABS plastic develops a shiny finish over time; PBT keycaps cost a little more, keep their texture for years, and are worth prioritising if you type a lot. Most keyboards let you swap keycaps freely, so this is easy to upgrade later.

The bottom line

For most people, the hot-swappable 75% board is the ideal entry point — compact, great-feeling, and future-proof. Save with the budget TKL to try mechanical, and step up to a gasket-mounted board once you're hooked. Check the current listing before buying, as prices and models change often.
